How to shoot a day-to-night time lapse with an Android phone

A practical guide · about 8 minutes

A sunset that rolls into the night is the most impressive time lapse you can shoot — and the hardest. In about an hour the light drops thousands of times. If the camera keeps the same exposure, the end of the clip turns black; if it adjusts automatically and abruptly, the video flickers. Here's how to get a smooth transition with nothing more than a phone and a tripod.

1. What you need

  • A tripod or any rock-solid support. The phone must not move by a millimetre for the whole shoot. A cheap phone clamp on a small tripod is enough; weigh it down if it's windy.
  • Power. A full battery and, for anything over an hour, a power bank.
  • Free storage. A 600-frame shoot at 12 MP takes roughly 2–3 GB before it becomes a video.
  • A clear view of the sky — ideally facing the sunset, with something interesting in the foreground: a skyline, mountains, water.

2. Do the interval math first

A time lapse is just photos taken at a fixed interval and played back as video. Three numbers decide everything:

frames = video length (s) × frame rate  and  shoot time = frames × interval

For a sunset, start about 30 minutes before the sun touches the horizon and keep going 30–45 minutes after it has set. The best colours often come 15–20 minutes after sunset.

3. Why exposure is the hard part

You have two options, and both fail on their own:

  • Fixed (manual) exposure: perfectly smooth, but by the time it's dark the frames are black.
  • Full auto exposure: the image stays bright, but the camera jumps between settings from frame to frame, which shows up as flicker.

Professional time-lapse photographers use a technique nicknamed the "Holy Grail": they keep exposure locked and change it in tiny, regular steps as the light fades — slower shutter speed first, then higher ISO. Each step is too small to see, so the video brightens and darkens smoothly instead of flickering. Traditionally that means a laptop or a dedicated controller attached to the camera.

On a phone: Steadylapse's Holy Grail mode (PRO, currently in beta) does this ramping automatically — it measures the scene and moves ISO and shutter speed a little at a time, frame by frame, within the limits your phone allows. When the phone reaches its longest allowed exposure, the clip simply darkens gently from there.

4. Lock everything else

Exposure isn't the only thing that flickers. Before you start:

  • Lock focus on something far away (or the horizon). If the phone refocuses between frames, the image subtly "breathes".
  • Lock white balance, or pick a fixed preset such as Daylight. Auto white balance shifts colour from frame to frame — and it would also neutralise the warm sunset tones you want.
  • Turn off HDR, beauty and scene modes in whatever app you use; they process each frame differently.

5. Keep the phone alive for an hour (or six)

  • Screen off. The screen is the biggest drain on the battery and a major source of heat. Use an app that keeps shooting with the screen off.
  • Heat. Phones throttle or shut the camera down when they get hot. Keep it out of direct sun, remove a thick case, and avoid charging in the heat if you can.
  • Interruptions. Silence the phone, and for overnight shoots switch off scheduled restarts and automatic app updates.

6. What phones can — and can't — do at night

Be realistic about the dark end of the sunset. Your phone's built-in night mode can expose for many seconds, but most phones limit how long a third-party app may expose a single frame — often well under a second. That's plenty for twilight, city lights and the moon, but usually not enough for the Milky Way. Plan the shot so that it ends at blue hour or with city lights rather than deep darkness, and you'll get a clean, bright result.

7. Shoot, then let it build

Start the sequence, turn the screen off and step away. Resist checking every few minutes — every touch is a chance to nudge the phone. When the last frame is captured, assemble the photos into a video at 24 or 30 fps.
